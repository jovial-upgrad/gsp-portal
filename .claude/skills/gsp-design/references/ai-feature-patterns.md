# AI feature representation, patterns and pitfalls

## The tells that read as generic or "AI slop"
The AI product design corner of Dribbble and much of the wider AI product landscape has converged on a recognisable, slightly generic look: a dark navy or near black base, an indigo or violet primary colour, a purple to blue gradient hero background, Inter as the typeface, and a bare sparkle icon standing in for "this is AI". This combination is now widely recognised across the design community as a tell rather than a strength, worth deliberately avoiding rather than defaulting to. The indigo specifically traces back to a popular UI kit's default button colour years ago, since copied so widely that it's become shorthand for "this was probably AI generated or AI templated". If a task needs to represent AI, that's a reason to reach for a colour and type choice distinct from this default, not a reason to reach for it.

## The sparkle icon problem specifically
Research testing the bare sparkle icon in isolation found that essentially no one reading it cold associated it with AI at all, guesses ranged across "what's new", a promotion, an accessibility feature, even unrelated symbols, and a meaningful share read it as a favourite or save action instead, which collides with the icon's much older, much more established meaning as a star. In context, next to other AI branded elements, people do learn to read it as an AI marker, but it should never be the only signal. Pair an AI marker with a text label and a colour treatment distinct from the product's own primary brand colour, and keep the icon used for an AI feature visually distinct from any avatar representing the AI itself.

## What actually tests well: copilot, not autopilot
The clearest pattern across current AI native products is a shift away from AI presented as an all knowing autopilot that acts unprompted, toward AI presented as a copilot, present, optional, and respectful of the user staying in control. A frequently cited example is an AI assistant helping with a recipe that responds to a specific question, "how do I make this dairy free", rather than unilaterally rewriting the page's content. The practical patterns that follow from this:
**Plan and execute**: show what the AI intends to do before it does it, rather than acting silently and only surfacing the result.
**Confidence signalling**: distinguish a high confidence action the AI can just take from a low confidence one that should be escalated to the user for a decision, rather than treating every AI action the same way.
**Progressive delegation**: offer a range of autonomy rather than a binary fully manual or fully automatic switch, letting a user hand over more control as trust builds rather than all at once.
**Always leave an undo, and show the reasoning or plan when it's not obvious.** A "black box" AI action with no visible reasoning and no way to reverse it is the pattern that erodes trust fastest. Worth noting too, people tend to trust an AI answer more when it shows citations, without reliably checking whether those citations actually support the claim, which is a reason to make sure citations shown are genuinely accurate rather than decorative.

## Named examples worth studying directly
**Coursera Coach**: an AI tutor embedded directly in the video player sidebar, summarising the lecture, explaining jargon in context, generating practice questions, and running a Socratic dialogue rather than just answering outright. Coursera has reported measurable learning gains tied to its use, a meaningfully higher first attempt quiz pass rate and more lessons completed per hour among learners who use it. This in-player, context aware tutor pattern is one of the closest available analogs for an AI feature inside a course asset page.
**Khan Academy's Khanmigo**: framed explicitly as a guide rather than a teacher, deliberately Socratic, asking a learner guiding questions rather than handing over the answer directly, which both builds trust and keeps the learner doing the actual thinking.
**Duolingo Max**: an AI powered "Roleplay" mode for practising conversation with a character, and an "Explain My Answer" feature giving context aware grammar and vocabulary feedback after a mistake, both wrapped in Duolingo's characteristically playful, high saturation, mascot led visual language rather than a serious "AI tool" aesthetic.
**Google Gemini and Google's AI Overviews**: use a consistent sparkle plus gradient container system, with a pulsing or flowing gradient specifically as the "thinking" or processing state, giving the user visible feedback that something is happening rather than a blank pause.

## Principles worth carrying into any AI feature design
Design for partnership, not replacement, the framing throughout the best examples above is "the AI helps you think", not "the AI thinks for you". Keep personalization and AI generated suggestions legible and not overwhelming, a clear next step beats a dense list of AI generated options. And show a visible processing or "thinking" state for anything that takes more than an instant, silence during an AI response reads as broken far more than it reads as fast.
