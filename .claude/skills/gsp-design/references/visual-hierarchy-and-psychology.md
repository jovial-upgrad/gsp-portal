# Visual hierarchy and design psychology

## Visual hierarchy, in order of how strongly each lever moves attention
**Size first.** A larger element gets noticed before anyone consciously reads it, this is the strongest, fastest lever available.
**Contrast second.** A dark element on a light field, or the reverse, wins the eye almost as fast as size does.
**Whitespace third.** Isolating an element, giving it room nothing else is competing for, reads as importance on its own.
**Alignment fourth.** A consistent grid gives the eye a path to follow through a layout, weaker alone than the first three but it's what makes a hierarchy feel intentional rather than accidental.
**Colour last, and the weakest lever used alone.** Colour is easy to reach for first, but it fails on a dim screen, fails for anyone with a colour vision deficiency, and does the least work of the five on its own. A useful check, if a layout still reads correctly in greyscale, the hierarchy underneath it was actually built with size, contrast, whitespace, and alignment, not just colour.

Supporting techniques worth knowing: a disciplined type scale (three levels is usually enough, heading, subhead, body) does more for perceived quality and hierarchy than most designers give it credit for, since it makes every screen in a product feel like part of the same system. Keep colour restrained, a muted base palette with exactly one reserved accent colour for the single primary action reads as considered, versus spreading several saturated colours across a screen which reads as noisy. Elevation, meaning shadows, blur, or a tint shift, should support the hierarchy already established by size and contrast, not be the only thing carrying it. And reading patterns matter, a text heavy page gets scanned in a rough F shape, while on a narrow mobile screen the vertical order of elements effectively is the hierarchy, so the most important message and its call to action belong first, not buried after supporting content.

## The Laws of UX worth reaching for most often
**Hick's Law**: more choices increase decision time, and not in a straight line, logarithmically, so a screen with meaningfully fewer options at the moment a decision needs to happen will feel faster even if the underlying feature set hasn't shrunk. Applies directly to any high intent screen, a checkout, a first run choice, a primary navigation.
**Fitts's Law**: the time it takes to reach a target depends on its size and its distance from wherever attention already is, so a primary action should be large and close at hand, not small and in a corner. A documented real world case, a 20 percent larger checkout button lifted click through by around 6 percent.
**Jakob's Law**: people bring expectations from every other product they've used, and prefer an interface that works like the ones they already know. A novel pattern needs to earn its novelty, the default should be familiar.
**Miller's Law**: working memory holds roughly seven items, plus or minus two, so no single screen should ask someone to actively hold more than about that many live choices or pieces of information at once, group and chunk anything larger.
**The Zeigarnik Effect**: people remember an unfinished task better than a finished one, which is the actual mechanism behind why progress bars, onboarding checklists, and "you're 80 percent done" style prompts work as well as they do.
**The Von Restorff Effect**: an item that visually stands out from everything around it is what people actually remember afterward, the real justification for giving exactly one primary call to action a genuinely different visual treatment rather than letting every button compete equally.
**The Doherty Threshold**: engagement and perceived quality rise sharply once a system responds within about 400 milliseconds, anything slower needs a visible progress or loading state or it reads as broken rather than just slow.
**The Aesthetic-Usability Effect**: people perceive a more attractive interface as more usable, even when it measurably isn't, which is a reason polish matters, but not a reason to let polish substitute for an interface that actually works.
**The Peak-End Rule**: people judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment and by how it ends, not by the average of the whole thing, worth remembering for anything with a clear completion moment, a course finished, a certificate earned, that moment deserves real design attention.

## How these connect to Nielsen's usability heuristics
The Laws of UX describe how people behave regardless of what gets designed. Nielsen's ten usability heuristics are an evaluation checklist built on top of that behaviour, for example "recognition rather than recall" rests directly on Miller's Law, and "consistency and standards" rests directly on Jakob's Law. The practical split, use the laws while designing, use the heuristics afterward to audit what got built.

## Learning specific psychology, relevant to any education product
**Cognitive load theory**: manage the load a learner is actually carrying by chunking and scaffolding content deliberately (intrinsic load, the complexity of the material itself), and by ruthlessly cutting anything in the interface that isn't necessary to understand it (extraneous load, an overcrowded screen, decoration competing with content, unnecessary interactivity bolted onto a simple concept). A distraction free reading view, showing interactive controls only when needed rather than all the time, is a concrete pattern that reduces extraneous load.
**Spaced repetition**: reintroducing content at deliberately increasing intervals measurably improves retention compared to massed, one time study, and this is well evidenced specifically in language learning products. The interface pattern that supports it: a review queue, a "due today" prompt, and a visible progress or streak indicator that sets accurate expectations about what's coming.
**Motivation, via self-determination theory**: motivation holds up best when a learner has some autonomy (real choices, not just one forced path), a visible sense of competence (mastery that increases through manageable, increasing challenge, not a flat difficulty curve), and some relatedness (a social or shared element, even a lightweight one). Gamification built on top of these three tends to work, streaks, levels, and instant feedback that's specific rather than generic. Gamification bolted on without them tends not to, worth remembering that gamification is not automatically positive, adding interactivity or immersion to a genuinely simple concept can increase extraneous cognitive load rather than motivation, match the mechanic to whether it's actually solving a motivation problem or just adding decoration.
